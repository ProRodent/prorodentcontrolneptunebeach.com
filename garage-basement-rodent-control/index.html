Why a garage is an easy target.

A garage offers almost everything a mouse or rat looks for: shelter from weather, quiet corners and stored materials that double as bedding. It often lacks the thing that discourages animals indoors, which is regular human activity.

Because many garages are also the first space a pest reaches, the problem can spread from there into the kitchen wall or the attic. Treating the garage early is often the cheapest step.

The checklist for a garage survey.

  • Bottom seal and corners of the door for light or worn rubber.
  • Joints between the slab and the walls.
  • Penetrations for pipes, wires and the air handler.
  • Shelving, with attention to the lowest shelf and anything on the floor.
  • Water heater closet and any utility alcove.
  • Attic hatch or ceiling access, if present.

A flashlight held low along the floor shows gaps better than a glance from standing height. Try it before the visit and tell us what you find.

Storage habits that help.

None of this needs a big purchase. A few tubs and an afternoon of rearranging change the room dramatically.

When the room is partly below grade.

Some garages and utility rooms sit lower than the rest of the house or open onto a slab that is partly below the surrounding ground. If yours does, mention it, since damp corners and drains give animals different options. The technician checks drains, vents and the base of the walls with extra care.

Coastal wear on garage doors.

Salt air and humidity can wear rubber seals and corrode hinges and brackets. We cannot say how fast that happens on your street, but a seal that looks cracked or flattened is worth replacing. Our sealing page describes the materials and the order of work.

What happens after the first visit.

Devices are set along the routes found, and the door is sealed once catches show the structure is clear. A recheck confirms the room is calm. Where the garage joins the house through a door or common wall, that seam is examined as well.

Interim chores.

  • List every pet, including the cat that wanders outside.
  • Wash thoroughly after touching anything near the mess, whether gloved or not.
  • Keep the foam can on the shelf, since a plugged hole hides the opening we need to measure.
  • Peek behind the water heater and beneath sinks with a flashlight and report back.

Pause here before any cleanup.

Sequence counts: ventilate, soak, wipe, bag, wash. Every line here lines up with the federal pages listed after this box.

  • Reach for the spray bottle before the dustpan; nothing is touched until it is thoroughly damp.
  • Air trapped in attics, garages and sheds needs a cross-breeze before anyone enters.
  • Gloves go on at the start of the job and come off at the very end, directly into the trash bag.
  • Dampen every patch you intend to handle, including the surface under the pellets.
  • Gather towels, gloves and waste in one bag, place it inside a second, and only then carry it out.
  • Hand washing ends the job, gloved or otherwise.

Where the cleanup guidance originates.

Each precaution in that box originates with a federal health or environmental office, not with our sales pitch.

  • CDC guidance for avoiding hantavirus: Fill holes, keep food in closed containers and clear away anywhere an animal might nest.
  • CDC guide to cleaning rodent-soiled rooms: How to handle stained surfaces, pellets and torn nesting with little airborne grit.

Details the dispatcher asks for.

  • Light you can see under the garage door.
  • Items resting on the floor or propped on walls.
  • Boxes that have not moved in a year.
  • Any sink, water heater or air handler in the room.

Questions about garages and storage rooms.

Why do garages attract rodents?

They are often dark, quiet and filled with boxes, bags of seed and cords. A worn door seal gives easy access, and nobody is there at night to disturb the animals.

Does a closed garage door keep them out?

Only if the seal is tight along the bottom and corners. A mouse needs only a pencil-width slot, and a rat can widen a narrower one.

Should I throw out the boxes?

Not necessarily. Move them off the floor, away from walls and into plastic bins where possible, and look at what is chewed first.

What if my garage is also a workshop?

Tell us what materials you keep and which tools run on power. Cords, insulation and sawdust each carry their own risks and fixes.

Do you handle sheds and carports?

Yes. The same survey applies to sheds, carports and outdoor storage rooms.

What to note about the room.

Does daylight show beneath the door? What is leaning on the walls? Which carton has stayed put for a year? Sheds, carports and utility rooms get the same questions.
