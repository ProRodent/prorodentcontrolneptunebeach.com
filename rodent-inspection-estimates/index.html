Why the survey comes first.

People often call asking for traps or a sealing crew. Both can be the right answer, but neither should be bought before someone has looked. A survey separates a one-mouse problem from a colony in the attic and a minor gap from a rotten soffit that needs a carpenter.

It also protects you from repeat work. Sealing a hole that animals are still using traps them inside, and setting devices without knowing the species wastes weeks.

The route the technician walks.

  • Outside first: foundation, roofline, vents, wires, utility sleeves, garage door and any palm or limb touching the house.
  • Garage and storage areas, including the lowest shelves and the corner behind the water heater.
  • Kitchen, laundry and bath cabinets where pipes enter the wall.
  • Attic or upper spaces, if they are reachable and safe.
  • Crawl space or under-floor access, where one exists.

Evidence is photographed and noted as the walk continues. If something is unsafe to enter, the report says so rather than guessing.

Getting the house ready.

Clear a path to the attic hatch and open the garage. Move the items stored against the walls you are most concerned about. Put pets in a room they cannot leave, since an open door and a curious dog complicate the lap outside.

What a survey cannot do.

A survey has no view inside a closed wall and cannot predict how many animals are left. It gives the best reading of the evidence, a plan and a price. When later work uncovers something new, the plan changes and you hear about it before any cost is added.

Interim chores.

  • Latch every bin lid tonight and roll the bins away from the garage wall.
  • If cleaning cannot wait, soak the spot with disinfectant before wiping with paper towels.
  • Raise the pet dishes onto a shelf overnight.
  • Crouch beside the garage door and note any strip of light along its base.

Federal cleanup steps, boiled down.

Dust from dried waste may carry hantavirus and other germs, so the routine below steers clear of anything that kicks it into the air.

  • A single dry dropping can crumble and release a cloud nobody notices.
  • Crack a window at opposite ends of the room to create a draft, and busy yourself elsewhere until time is up.
  • Gloves go on at the start of the job and come off at the very end, directly into the trash bag.
  • Make a fresh bleach mix for each session, since a stale batch loses strength.
  • Pets stay clear of the tied bag until it is inside the outdoor bin.
  • If a fever or muscle aches appear in the next few weeks, mention the rodent exposure to a doctor.

Questions about the survey.

How long does the survey take?

Duration depends on building size and how many spaces can be reached. A small house takes less time than a large one, and a shop with several rooms and a stock area takes longer still.

Do I need to be home?

Someone with access should be there, or you can arrange entry in advance. The technician needs the attic hatch, the garage and any crawl space door opened.

What if you find nothing?

Then the written note says so, and it lists the places checked. That record helps if signs return, and it spares you from paying for work that is not needed.

Is the estimate binding?

The estimate lists proposed steps and prices based on what was found. If something new appears during the work, the technician explains it and asks before changing the scope.

Can landlords or property managers book this?

Yes. Name the person who approves the work and the one holding keys, and we schedule around both.

Getting ready for the survey.
