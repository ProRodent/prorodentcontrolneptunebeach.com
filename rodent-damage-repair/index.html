Typical targets for gnawing.

Mice and rats chew to keep their teeth short. They gnaw through insulation, plastic pipe, flex duct, wire sheathing, wood and even soft metals such as lead. Damage often shows up as a hole where there should not be one or as an air handler that struggles.

Not every sign is rodent damage. Termites, water and age also leave marks, so the technician checks the pattern before naming the cause.

The repair sequence.

  • Check that nothing lives inside any longer and every entry is shut.
  • Photograph and list the damage by room.
  • Remove contaminated material and clean the space.
  • Install new insulation, duct sections or patches.
  • Test airflow and look for leftover gaps.
  • Note any damage that belongs to another trade.

Skipping steps one and three wastes money. The cleanup page explains the order.

Moisture and air handlers in humid climates.

Air handler closets and duct runs are common sites of damage because they are warm, hidden and close to the cooling system. Damp insulation around ducts can look like rodent damage, so we check for condensation and leaks as well. If the cause is water, we say so and refer it to the right trade.

Working with other contractors.

Some repairs cross trades. A roofer may need to fix a rotten fascia, an electrician may need to replace wire and a HVAC technician may need to service ducts. We keep notes that each can use, and we coordinate access where we can.

Final walk-through.

After repairs, the technician walks the space with you. You see the new material, the sealed entries and the notes for any outstanding items. A copy goes with your estimate for future reference.

Small jobs for the interim.

  • Keep interior doors shut so the trouble stays in one room.
  • A dead animal should never touch bare skin; gloves and a knotted bag are the minimum.
  • Keep furniture away from the wall that is making noise.
  • Photograph gnawed cable from arm's length and stay clear of any bare copper.

What the public health pages advise.

Once rodent waste dries it flakes into fine grit, which is why careful cleaning is wet and unhurried. The advice below mirrors public health guidance, whether or not you ever hire us.

  • A single dry dropping can crumble and release a cloud nobody notices.
  • Where a room has been shut, prop the doors and windows open and wait half an hour outside.
  • Nest material is never a barehanded job, however dry it appears.
  • After the wait, lift the soaked mess in a single sweep with a paper towel.
  • Sponges, mops and brushes used during the job belong in the trash as well.
  • Hand washing ends the job, gloved or otherwise.

Things to jot down first.

  • Pictures of chewed insulation, ducts or wire jackets.
  • Any rooms with stains, holes or loose drywall.
  • Whether the air handler has been running poorly.
  • Who owns or manages the space.

Repair queries.

Do you handle electrical repairs?

We document chewed wiring and refer the repair to a licensed electrician. Our report helps them find the damage.

Why do repairs wait until the end?

New material placed while animals are active gets damaged again. Trapping and sealing come first.

Can I use my own contractor?

Yes. Our notes list the damage and the likely materials, which your contractor can price and perform.

How do I know what is rodent damage?

Teeth leave small parallel grooves and ragged edges. The technician photographs examples and explains how to tell them from other wear.

Does insurance cover this?

Policies differ. Ask your insurer how they treat animal damage, and keep our report and photos for your claim.

Scope of a repair visit.

Take photos of chewed insulation, ducting and wire jackets, and stay clear of any bare copper.
