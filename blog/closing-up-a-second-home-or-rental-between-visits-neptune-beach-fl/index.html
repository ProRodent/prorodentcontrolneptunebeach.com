Closing Up a Second Home or Rental Between Visits

A house that stands empty for weeks may harbor a colony unseen. Below is a closing routine for second homes and rentals that makes the place less appealing.

An empty house is quiet, dark and still. That is just what a mouse is looking for. Without people, nobody hears the scratching, notices the first droppings or sweeps up crumbs. When the owner or guest arrives, the animals have settled, and a small problem has grown.

This article is for owners and managers of second homes and short-term rentals in beach communities. It describes a closing routine and a checking routine. Nothing here is a claim about how many such houses have problems. It is general advice that fits any property that sits vacant for stretches.

Why vacancy matters.

  • No noise or light to keep animals cautious.
  • Food left behind by guests, in cabinets, bins or the garage.
  • Doors and windows opened and closed without checking the seals.
  • Plumbing and air conditioning running, which keeps the house comfortable.
  • Gaps that nobody has time to find.

Rental turnover adds more risk. Guests may leave food, bins may overflow, and cleaning crews may prop doors open. None of this is anyone's fault. It is the pattern of a house used on and off.

The closing checklist.

Area.Do before leaving.Why.
Kitchen and pantry.Remove perishables; move dry goods to hard containers; wipe counters.Takes away the meal.

Print the list and keep a copy in a drawer so each closing follows the same steps.

The checking routine.

Whoever checks the house between stays should carry a flashlight and a short list. A ten-minute walk is enough: kitchen, pantry, garage, under the sinks and the exterior. Look for droppings, gnawed wrappers, shredded nest scraps and a musty or ammonia smell.

  • Photograph anything unusual, with a coin for scale.
  • Note the date and location in a shared log.
  • Leave droppings unswept and unvacuumed, and phone us instead.
  • Report open doors, torn screens or damaged seals right away.

Rentals and guest habits.

A short, friendly note for guests can cut risk. Ask them to take out trash before they leave, store food in sealed containers and keep doors shut. Leave a number to call if they hear or see anything unusual.

After each stay, the manager or cleaner can do a quick check. If signs appear, they can call, and a survey shows what is needed before the next guests arrive.

What to do if you find signs.

Do not rush to clean. Photograph, avoid stirring dust and call. For small amounts, the CDC steps in the box below describe the damp-wipe method. For anything larger, a crew with protective gear is the right choice, as the attic cleanup page explains.

Never seal holes while animals may be inside. They can chew a new exit, and an animal that dies in a wall adds an odor problem.

Seal first, then relax.

The strongest protection for a vacant house is a tight envelope. A sealing visit addresses the roofline, vents, door seals and pipe entries with metal materials. It does not need to be repeated every season, though a yearly look at the seals is wise.

A schedule that works.

  • Before leaving: complete the closing list.
  • Within two weeks: a caretaker visit with the checking list.
  • After each guest: a quick check and a trash walk.
  • Once a year: a look at roofline, vents and door seals.

The routine is simple, and it moves the effort from emergencies to habits.

Pause here before any cleanup.

None of this is medical advice. Think of it as a brief list of habits that public health pages recommend when rodents have been in a home.

  • A single dry dropping can crumble and release a cloud nobody notices.
  • Air trapped in attics, garages and sheds needs a cross-breeze before anyone enters.
  • Keep a spare pair nearby so no pellet ever meets bare skin.
  • The liquid needs roughly five minutes on the surface, because the standing time is what disinfects.
  • Pets stay clear of the tied bag until it is inside the outdoor bin.
  • Work the lather under your nails and up the wrists, then dry on a clean towel.

Questions from readers.

How often should an empty house be checked?

Every week or two is a sensible target. A neighbor, manager or cleaning service can do it with a list. Check inside and out, and look for the clues described here.

Should I leave food in the pantry?

Remove everything perishable and put dry goods in hard containers. Leave nothing open, and empty the fridge if it will be off.

Do traps help while nobody is home?

Not without a plan. A trap left unchecked can leave a dead animal for weeks. Better to prevent entry and arrange regular checks.

What about tenants who leave food out?

Provide a simple written list of dos and don'ts, and check after each stay. A short note about trash and food storage helps.

Can you check the property for me?

We can survey it and report on gaps and signs. For regular checks, ask a manager or caretaker, and share our findings with them.
