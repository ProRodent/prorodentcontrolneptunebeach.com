Position counts for more than quantity.

A trap in the middle of a room catches a mouse mostly by accident. A trap flush against a baseboard where pellets are piled catches it on purpose. The same holds for rats, which prefer to run along beams, fence tops and the lines where walls meet floors.

That is why the technician reads the evidence before opening a single box. Grease marks show the lane, pellet clusters show the rest stops, and gnawing shows the next obstacle.

The choice is made on site. Using one style everywhere would be simpler, and it would catch fewer animals.

Scheduling the rechecks.

The first recheck usually comes a few days after placement, once the first catches show where the live routes run. Later visits space out as catches slow. Each trip empties devices, rebaits where needed and moves any device that has gone quiet.

  • The log lists device type, location and date placed.
  • Catches are counted and compared with the earlier visit.
  • Gaps that the catches expose are noted for sealing.
  • Devices are removed from the property when the job closes.

Once the routes are known, the sealing page shows how entries get closed.

Keeping the area workable.

Devices need quiet to work. Keep pets and children away from marked areas, avoid moving a device to see if it caught something and call if you see one that has been disturbed. Leaving your own traps in the same room confuses the pattern.

Why sticky boards stay in the box.

Glue boards catch lizards, small birds and anything else that walks over them. A trapped mouse can also drag a board into a hidden spot, where it dies and leaves an odor. For those reasons, our plans do not use them.

When the job is finished.

A job closes when devices stay empty through the agreed recheck period and the seals hold under pressure. At that point everything we placed is removed and the final note lists what was done. If signs return later, the log helps the next visit start from knowledge, not guesses.

Interim chores.

  • Clear a three-foot strip along the noisiest wall.
  • Leave any trap that has caught something where it lies, and mention it on the call.
  • Aerosol sprayed into a wall may only push the animal deeper.
  • Tape a card beside the phone for questions you want answered.

Questions about traps.

Are the traps safe around pets and children?

Devices go out of reach or into locked stations, and you get a tour of every spot. Tell us about every pet, including cats that roam outdoors.

Why use traps instead of bait?

A trapped animal can be counted and removed. A baited animal may die inside a wall, creating odor and cleanup work. Consumer-grade bait carries limits, which the EPA page linked below lays out.

How many traps will you set?

The number follows the evidence. A single mouse route may need two or three, and an attic with active rats may need more.

Why do traps sometimes sit empty for days?

Rats are wary of new objects, so early quiet is normal. Empty devices on a strong route often catch something once the animals settle.

Where do trapped animals go afterward?

They leave the property and are disposed of under local rules. Handling them is never your job.

Where devices will go.

Point out fallen fronds, stacked pavers or a shed pressed near the walls, since runs often begin there. The approach is identical in every covered place.
