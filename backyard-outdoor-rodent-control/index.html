The last twenty feet.

Rodents rarely cross open lawn if they can avoid it. They prefer to move along a fence, a hedge, a wall or the underside of stacked material. The stretch from the yard edge to your wall is where the trip becomes visible and where simple changes pay off.

We have no survey of burrow numbers around Neptune Beach, so nothing here describes any one yard. The technician reads what is on your lot and starts there.

Parks, green strips and open ground.

If your lot borders a park or an open strip, such as the Jarboe Park named in our local notes, we know nothing about conditions there and claim none. The general point is that cover at a boundary shapes the first approach. Mention it on the call and the technician checks that edge of your property first.

Sheds, decks and outdoor storage.

A shed sitting on the ground or a deck low enough to crawl under gives animals shelter and a hidden path to the house. Look underneath with a flashlight. If you see burrow mouths, droppings or flattened soil, take photos. The garage page covers the interior side of the same problem.

  • Skirting or mesh at the base of decks and sheds.
  • Compost kept in a closed bin, not an open pile.
  • Gutters and drains cleared so water does not pool by walls.
  • Pet food and trash kept inside or in closed bins overnight.

Why not just poison them?

Loose bait outdoors can reach pets, birds and other wildlife, and animals that eat it often die where nobody can find them. The EPA page linked below explains how consumer products are restricted. Locked stations and traps keep the work controlled and countable.

How long outdoor work takes.

A first yard pass can fit into the same visit as the survey. Follow-up trips check devices and note whether burrows are still in use. Weather and lot size change the pace, so timing is agreed on the call; the phone is answered day and night.

From call to doorbell.

  • Keep interior doors shut so the trouble stays in one room.
  • A dead animal should never touch bare skin; gloves and a knotted bag are the minimum.
  • Keep furniture away from the wall that is making noise.
  • Photograph gnawed cable from arm's length and stay clear of any bare copper.

Handling mouse and rat mess: five precautions.

Sequence counts: ventilate, soak, wipe, bag, wash. Every line here lines up with the federal pages listed after this box.

  • A single dry dropping can crumble and release a cloud nobody notices.
  • Ventilating comes first in every official checklist, and thirty minutes is the usual guide.
  • Choose gloves with long cuffs, because grit tends to settle at the wrist.
  • Make a fresh bleach mix for each session, since a stale batch loses strength.
  • A second bag keeps everything sealed in until collection.
  • Hand washing ends the job, gloved or otherwise.

Questions about yards.

Can I stop rodents just by cleaning the yard?

Cleanup reduces food and cover, which helps, but it rarely stops them alone. Sealing the house and trapping complete the picture.

Are bird feeders a draw for rodents?

They may be. Spilled seed is an easy meal, and platform feeders near walls pull animals close. Shifting feeders away and adding a catch tray helps.

What about fallen palm fronds?

Piles of fronds and other yard debris may shelter animals, so removing them from beside walls is a sensible step. We cannot say how much any one pile matters.

Should I fill burrows myself?

Please call first. Filling a burrow with animals inside pushes them somewhere else, often toward your house.

Do you work near parks and open ground?

Yes. If your property borders a park or green strip, say so, because cover at the boundary shapes where the first devices go.

Yard facts that help us plan.

Describe the fence line, any shrubs leaning on the walls and where you saw something move. Small beach-side lots and larger ones both work; the plan adjusts.
